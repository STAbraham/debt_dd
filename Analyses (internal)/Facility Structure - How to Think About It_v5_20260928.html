Accial's website describes its house structure as "bankruptcy-remote vehicles, controlled cash accounts, and recourse to the originator."

The facts every structure must reconcile: the lenders' dollars sit offshore (FPF in Panama; Accial lends USD); the collateral is PHP credit-card receivables owned by ZFPI, a BSP-supervised non-bank card issuer; our parent is Zed Financial, Inc. (Delaware) and there is no Singapore entity today; and cash must cross the Philippine border both ways.

This is Zed's first structured credit facility. That fact shapes everything below. We have no borrowing history, no servicer track record a lender can point to, and a ~$4M book seeking a ~$5M commitment — small enough that fixed structuring costs (SPV formation and upkeep, security registration, legal opinions, lender counsel) are a material fraction of the facility.

Two consequences: (i) the structure must be sized to the deal — a first facility is not the place to pay for architecture we will not need until $20M+; and (ii) lenders will price the absence of track record with recourse to us, not just to the receivables — the negotiation is about the form and cap of that recourse, not its existence.

The design goal is a first facility that is cheap to close, proves us as a borrower and servicer, and carries its borrowing-base plumbing into a larger second facility (offshore or onshore PHP) without rebuilding.

Three decisions define any proposal: who borrows (§4), how the lender reaches the receivables (§5), and therefore what product it is — asset-backed or venture debt (§6). Tax (§7) and balance-sheet/guarantee (§8) follow. §3 sets the market baseline for our stage; §9 shows what Philippine peers have done; §11 is the recommended posture.

2. Basics — the concepts behind the jargon

What a lender owns. A loan is an unsecured promise to pay. If the borrower fails, the lender stands in line with every other unpaid creditor and shares what is left. Security (collateral) is how a lender jumps the line: a legal interest in a specific asset that it can take or sell on default and be paid from first. The structure debate is therefore about which asset the lender gets that first claim on, and how many other claims stand between the lender and the cash that actually comes from cardholders.

OpCo, HoldCo, SPV — the three kinds of entity in every structure. An operating company (OpCo) runs the business: holds the licence, employs the people, owns the operating assets. Ours is ZFPI — the BSP-supervised card issuer that owns the receivables and the cardholder relationships. A holding company (HoldCo) owns shares in other companies and operates nothing itself. Ours is Zed Financial, Inc. (Delaware), whose main asset is 100% of ZFPI plus the cash from equity raises. A special-purpose vehicle (SPV) is a shell created for one transaction, owned by the HoldCo and sitting beside the OpCo, whose only asset is its claim on a defined pool of receivables and whose only creditor is the lender.

Why the labels matter: lend to the OpCo and security can sit directly on the operating assets, but every step runs through the regulator. Lend to the HoldCo and the natural collateral is shares of the OpCo — worth only what is left after the OpCo's own creditors are paid — unless extra machinery (an assigned intercompany note, or direct OpCo security) is added; in exchange the HoldCo is unregulated, sits in a treaty jurisdiction, and can be enforced against outside Philippine courts. Lend to an SPV and the lender gets a borrower with no other creditors, at the cost of forming and running one more entity. When Carrie says "holdco corporate guarantee" she means Zed Financial, Inc. standing behind whichever entity borrows. An intermediate HoldCo (e.g. a Singapore company inserted between the parent and ZFPI) is a fourth possibility this note does not pursue: it requires transferring ZFPI's shares, with Philippine and US tax consequences.

Ranking. On insolvency, claims are paid in fixed order: secured creditors from their collateral, then unsecured creditors pro rata, then shareholders. "Ranks alongside" means shares pro rata. "Senior" means the lender's debt ranks ahead of any other debt and all equity in the borrower's queue — on a first facility that is mostly a promise about the future, enforced by negative-pledge and no-other-debt covenants that stop the group putting anyone in front of the lender later.

Each legal entity has its own queue: ZFPI's creditors are paid from ZFPI's assets; the Delaware parent's creditors from the parent's assets. The parent does not own the receivables — it owns shares of ZFPI, which are worth only what remains after ZFPI's own creditors are paid.

Share pledge (share charge). The parent gives the lender a security interest in the ZFPI shares it holds. On default the lender can take or sell the shares — i.e. take control of ZFPI as a company. It is not a claim on the receivables: the lender becomes ZFPI's owner, and owners are last in ZFPI's own queue.

If ZFPI is itself insolvent — the moment a lender most needs its security — the shares can be worth nothing. A share pledge protects against the parent misbehaving, not against the assets failing. That is why a share-pledge-only deal is venture debt whatever the term sheet calls it.

Why an SPV changes the ranking picture. If FPF lends to the Delaware parent, it sits in the parent's queue with every other parent creditor — a later venture-debt bank, a litigant, an unpaid vendor. The documents fight over that (negative pledges, intercreditor agreements), but FPF's risk is "Zed the company."

If FPF lends to a special-purpose vehicle whose only asset is its claim on a ring-fenced receivables pool and whose only creditor is FPF — the documents forbid it from borrowing elsewhere, hiring, or doing any other business — there is no queue. Nothing that happens at the parent puts a competing claim inside the SPV. FPF's risk is "the pool, plus the mechanics that move cash from ZFPI to the SPV." That is isolation, and it is why lenders accept a thinner guarantee and a higher advance rate for it.

The catch: unless the SPV actually buys the receivables (a true sale), ZFPI still owns them and the SPV is merely ZFPI's secured creditor, so a ZFPI insolvency still reaches FPF — with a much shorter queue than a parent-level loan.

"Fast share-charge enforcement under Singapore law." A charge over a Singapore company's shares normally carries a private power of sale: on default the chargee sells or takes the shares without a court order (Conveyancing and Law of Property Act §24 for mortgages by deed; market practice adds blank transfers, irrevocable proxies and undated director resignations so the switch is mechanical).

In the Philippines, by contrast, enforcing security is a court-heavy process and a FRIA stay order can freeze it for months. Singapore's reliability makes the offshore layer predictable — if things go wrong, the fund takes the SPV and with it the SPV's secured loan into ZFPI.

Two nuances the research turned up: (i) Singapore's restructuring moratorium (IRDA §64) can restrain enforcement of security against the SPV if the SPV itself applies for protection — but a charge granted by the Delaware parent over the SPV's shares is the parent's property and is not stayed by the SPV's moratorium.

(ii) IRDA §440 (ipso facto) bars accelerating a loan "by reason only" of the borrower's insolvency filing — plain loan agreements are not carved out — but acceleration on payment or covenant default remains available. Neither speeds up anything inside the Philippines.

Guarantee vs security. Security is a claim on an asset; a guarantee is a third party's promise to pay if the borrower does not. Accial's "holdco corporate guarantee" puts the parent's whole balance sheet back behind the SPV — which gives back most of the isolation benefit.

The negotiated middle: the parent guarantees it will not cheat (fraud, misrepresenting the pool, failing to service — "bad-boy" carve-outs) but does not guarantee that cardholders pay.

First loss vs guarantee. Both put Zed's money at risk; they are different mechanisms and a lender will usually ask for both. First loss is structural and sits inside the facility: at an 80% advance rate the lender funds ₱80 of every ₱100 of eligible receivables and Zed funds the other ₱20 (equity or a junior note); cardholder defaults eat Zed's ₱20 before touching the lender's ₱80. It is automatic, continuous, sized as a percentage of the pool, and capped by construction — the most Zed can lose through it is its slice of the pool. A guarantee is contractual and sits outside the facility: the parent promises to pay if the borrower cannot, triggered by a shortfall at the borrower level, and it reaches the parent's own cash, which first loss never does.

The sequence on a bad pool: cardholder losses burn Zed's first-loss slice → losses exceed it and start hitting the lender's advance → the borrower cannot cover the shortfall → the lender calls the guarantee up to its cap → beyond the cap, the lender eats it. The lender's true exposure is therefore losses beyond (first loss + guarantee cap). One trap: an obligation to repurchase receivables that go delinquent or fall out of eligibility moves credit losses back to Zed with no cap — a de facto unlimited guarantee that nobody calls one. Read a term sheet's repurchase clause as carefully as its guarantee clause.

On/off balance sheet. A legal statement (the debt is not the parent's obligation; the assets are not the parent's) that is often misheard as an accounting one.

Under both IFRS 10 and US GAAP (ASC 810), a sponsor that keeps first loss and servicing consolidates the SPV; ASC 860 derecognition additionally requires legal isolation (a true-sale opinion) and ceded control. A secured loan to a wholly-owned SPV is on the parent's consolidated balance sheet under either framework.

Borrowing base. The facility's drawable amount = an advance rate (say 80–85%) × the balance of eligible receivables (e.g. under 30 days past due, within concentration limits), recomputed monthly. If the pool shrinks or sours, the borrower repays down to the new cap. This is what makes a facility "asset-backed" regardless of which entity signs.

3. What is common for a lender of our nature and stage

Sources: lender websites and published terms (Accial, Lendable, CIM, Helicap, Cauris/ALMA), the World Bank/CGAP 2025 focus note on fintech debt in emerging markets, the FMO/Dalberg 2026 landscape study, US warehouse-market primers (a16z, Mayer Brown, DLA Piper 2025), and disclosed first facilities in the region. Direct answers to the three questions:

3.1 Can we isolate the receivables in an SPV at our stage?

Yes — but understand whose tool it is. The SPV is the lender's risk-isolation device, not a borrower perk, and in emerging markets it comes paired with recourse to us, not instead of it. Accial's stated standard is "bankruptcy-remote vehicles, controlled cash accounts, and recourse to the originator"; the World Bank note adds that Accial's SPV setup is there to pool assets and allow "switching loan servicers" if needed.

CIM lends only through securitised SPVs but its facilities "typically range from $25 million to $150 million" — below its floor for us. Lendable's default is the opposite: "we provide balance-sheet debt and can structure SPVs" — mainly senior secured loans of $5–50M (average ~$7M), and it is the first offshore institutional lender to 70% of its borrowers. Helicap ($2–50M) and ALMA ($1–20M) describe senior secured loans, no SPV. Cauris runs a "graduation" model: small unsecured first, senior secured later.

No SPV-based first facility at $3–15M has been publicly disclosed anywhere in the region. The disclosed first facilities were all no-SPV structures — the lender lent straight to an existing group company and took security over the loan book:

  • BillEase — $20M from Lendable (2022): borrower was the Philippine OpCo, First Digital Finance Corp (the SEC-licensed financing company that originates the BNPL loans); "secured debt" — the CEO described it as collateralising the loan book. No SPV.
  • Salmon — $20M from Argentem Creek (2023): borrower was the HoldCo, Salmon Group Ltd (Abu Dhabi/ADGM), which pushed the money down into its Philippine financing companies and bank. No SPV — the HoldCo itself was the borrower, the equivalent of Zed Financial, Inc. borrowing. Its security (as later disclosed in the bond terms) was HoldCo-style: share pledges over the PH companies, guarantees from the PH financing companies, and pledged intragroup loans — no disclosed lien on the loan book. So Salmon is a data point for HoldCo borrowing, not for asset-backed structure (see §5).
  • ErudiFi — $15M from Helicap (2022): borrower was the Singapore-headquartered HoldCo above the Philippine (Bukas) and Indonesian (Danacita) OpCos, per Helicap's release; the exact signing entity is not named. No SPV disclosed.
  • Uploan — up to $15M from Lendable (2021; $6M committed up front): borrower was the Singapore HoldCo, Uploan Asia Pte Ltd, above the Philippine OpCo Financing Gate PH (the SEC-licensed financing company that originates the salary loans and owns the receivables). No SPV — the existing HoldCo was the offshore entity Lendable lent to. Lendable's words: on-balance-sheet (nothing was sold — the receivables stayed on Financing Gate's books, the facility sits as a liability on the group's), senior secured (ranks first; holds collateral), with security "in compliance with the 2020 regulations" — i.e. the PPSA's implementing rules. Because the borrower was a HoldCo with no assets of its own, "secured" can only mean the rung 2–3 machinery of §5 reaching down into the OpCo (receivables security, share pledge, assigned intercompany loan) — inferred from how a HoldCo loan must be secured, not itemised in the release. One historical caveat: the PPSA registry did not exist in 2021 (the PPSR went live 3 Feb 2025), so "PPSA-compliant" then meant drafted under the Act with perfection through the interim chattel-mortgage route; a facility Zed signs now perfects by a registry filing — a materially better position than Uploan had.

In other words, the regional first-facility pattern is exactly the "Delaware parent borrows" or "ZFPI borrows" rows of §4 — a HoldCo or OpCo loan and, where the borrower is the HoldCo, share pledges and intragroup-loan assignments to reach the OpCo (rungs 3–4 in §5), with receivables security disclosed only in the Uploan deal and the onshore bank lines. SPVs appear later and larger: Atome's HSBC-led receivables facility ($100M→$345M) and its Lending Ark "asset-backed" line are the SPV-style deals, and Atome's Singapore HoldCo had been borrowing for years before them.

The closest public analogue to our size and book ratio is Aflore (Colombia) — $10M from Accial in 2020 on ~$16M cumulative originations — almost certainly SPV-based given Accial's model, though the release does not say.

Size-vs-complexity evidence. No source states a hard threshold.

Directionally: the World Bank note says asset-backed tickets "may not be as low as" other products because of "additional costs related to creating an SPV and working with a portfolio servicing company"; FMO finds transaction costs fall materially above $5M and that collateral registration alone "can reach 2.5% of loan value for transactions under USD 5 million"; Stirling's warehouse primer contrasts on-balance-sheet ("simpler setup, fewer moving parts") with SPV ("preferred by institutional lenders for risk isolation" but adds "complexity, custody requirements, and servicing obligations"); a16z: bank warehouse groups want $20M+ of year-one originations and securitisation needs ~$150M.

Read-across for us: at $5M an SPV is justified only if the lender's model requires it (Accial) or if its running cost is trivial (a Delaware LLC — §4); it is not something to pay a Singapore structure for on our own initiative.

3.2 Will every lender want a parent guarantee and share recourse?

Expect a guarantee; do not expect the US-style "bad-acts-only" version on a first facility; resist the opco share pledge. In the US warehouse market the norm is well documented — the lender lends to a bankruptcy-remote SPV "with limited or no recourse to the parent" and takes a limited guaranty covering only fraud, wilful misconduct, voluntary bankruptcy, unauthorised transfers and separateness/eligibility breaches (Mayer Brown 2023; DLA Piper Oct 2025).

DLA Piper describes the alternative as a capped payment guaranty, "typically ten percent of the committed amount," with "negotiated sunsets and step-downs," and warns that repurchase obligations triggered by borrowing-base deficiencies "operate as a de facto guarantee."

Emerging-market reality is different: Accial pairs the SPV with recourse to the originator; FMO/Dalberg: "many companies take initial debt facilities which fully collateralize their assets"; the World Bank's 4G Capital case shows a profitable lender with a debt history getting a $3M bank revolver only with a US-government guarantee, then 50% cash collateral.

No public EM source documents a bad-acts-only guarantee or a guarantee step-down after 12 months — those are negotiating asks [practice], not market norms.

Share pledge over the opco is nowhere described as standard for an asset-backed facility; it is a holdco/venture-debt feature (Salmon's bond, Argentem Creek). In SPV deals the parent pledges the SPV's equity, which is a different thing — it hands the lender the ring-fenced structure, not the operating company.

Realistic first-facility ask: parent guarantee capped (10% of commitment is the documented US reference point; expect the lender to open higher for an EM first-timer), with a step-down or fall-away after 12–18 months of clean borrowing-base compliance.

Pledge of the SPV/borrower equity yes, pledge of ZFPI shares no (offer direct ZFPI receivables security — rung 2 — as the stronger substitute). "Limited recourse confined to the SPV and its assets" is a second-facility outcome, not a first-facility one.

5. How the lender reaches the receivables — the recourse ladder

ZFPI owns the receivables. Whatever entity signs, the lender's real position is set by which rung it sits on. Strongest first:

  1. True sale to an SPV. ZFPI sells receivables arising on designated accounts; the SPV borrows senior from the fund, we hold the junior first-loss piece; collections run through a waterfall. Legally isolated from a ZFPI insolvency. Hard here: the card account stays with the licensed issuer; a Securitization Act (RA 9267) route needs an SEC-approved plan and prior BSP endorsement because ZFPI is BSP-supervised; the buyer's discount/yield on the purchased receivables (not their face value) is subject to 12% VAT or 5% gross receipts tax depending on who the buyer is [counsel — no BIR ruling on card-receivable sales found]; a revolving purchase program run by a foreign SPV raises "doing business in the Philippines" questions under the Foreign Investments Act (isolated transactions are excluded; a continuing program is riskier) [counsel]. Chambers' 2026 Philippines guide: the legal framework exists but "the securitisation market has not yet developed." No Philippine fintech has ever disclosed one (§9). Legal cost well into six figures — heavy for $5M. Whether the BSP permits the sale at all is answered in §5.1 (it does; the obstacles are FX and tax).
  2. ZFPI grants security directly to the lender. Upstream guarantee plus a security interest over receivables and collection accounts. Now materially easier than a year ago: the Personal Property Security Act registry (PPSR) went live 3 Feb 2025, perfection is by registered notice, and the Act allows direct collection of receivables (§48) and commercially reasonable private disposition (§49) without court process. Upstream guarantees are permitted if authorized in the articles and for demonstrable corporate benefit (the proceeds being on-lent to ZFPI supplies it); no statutory financial-assistance prohibition. No BSP restriction on a non-bank card issuer encumbering receivables was found in RA 10870, Circular 1003 or the MORNBFI CC-Regulations (§5.1) — the remaining check is whether ZFPI's Certificate of Authority carries bespoke conditions [counsel].
  3. Limited-recourse secured intercompany loan, assigned to the lender. ZFPI secures a loan from the borrower (parent, Singapore SPV or US SPV) with the pool; the borrower assigns that secured note to the fund. On default the fund steps into the lender-of-record position against ZFPI. The plumbing is visible in Salmon's bond terms (pledged intragroup loans are how a HoldCo lender reaches an OpCo at all), but Salmon's intragroup loans are not disclosed as secured on receivables — see the note below — so rung 3 proper requires the extra step Zed would add: ZFPI grants receivables security to back the intercompany note. This matches Carrie's 8/19 description and Accial's "recourse to the originator" language.
  4. Share pledge over ZFPI only. The fund ranks behind every ZFPI creditor. Venture debt.

The rungs are combined in real deals, not chosen one at a time. A lender to the Delaware parent would typically take rung 3 (secured intercompany note assigned) and rung 4 (ZFPI share pledge) and often rung 2 (direct ZFPI security). A guarantee from the OpCo, or a pledged-but-unsecured intragroup loan, sits between rungs 3 and 4: it puts the lender in the OpCo's creditor queue rather than behind it, but with no lien on the receivables. The posture in §11 — concede a pledge of the borrower/SPV equity, refuse a pledge of ZFPI shares, offer rung 2 instead — is about which rungs to pair.

Reading Salmon's package on this ladder. The bond terms disclose share pledges over the PH financing companies and bank (rung 4), guarantees from the PH financing companies (unsecured OpCo claims), and pledged intragroup loans (the rung 3 mechanism, but with no disclosed receivables security behind the loans). That is rung 4 plus unsecured OpCo-level claims — a HoldCo bond with recourse to the operating companies, not an asset-backed facility — which is consistent with its 15% bond pricing rather than borrowing-base economics, and with §6's point that a share-pledge-led package is venture-style debt whatever it is called. Earlier versions of this note described it as "i.e. rung 3"; that overstated it.

On rungs 2–4 a Philippine rehabilitation Commencement Order (FRIA §16) suspends enforcement of security; the lien is not impaired (§60) and the court may permit foreclosure if the asset is not needed for rehabilitation, but the delay is real. Lenders price it with tighter covenants, fuller guarantees and sometimes warrants.

5.1 Does the BSP allow ZFPI to sell receivables to a Singapore or US SPV?

Short answer: yes — there is no BSP prohibition and no BSP prior approval, provided the sale is done as an ordinary assignment of credit and stays outside the Securitization Act. The obstacles are practical (FX repatriation, tax leakage, "doing business" exposure), not regulatory. Findings below are from the primary texts (RA 10870, BSP Circular 1003 and the MORNBFI "CC-Regulations", RA 9267, the Civil Code, RA 11057, RA 10173, the BSP FX Manual May 2025); law-firm commentary and SEC/NPC opinions could not be retrieved, so points marked [counsel] need confirmation.

  • Card-issuer rules (RA 10870; Circular 1003; MORNBFI Sec. 101-CC). A full-text search finds nothing on sale, transfer, assignment or securitisation of receivables, and no "material change in business" approval. The only post-licensing Monetary Board approval is for voluntary cessation of the card business — a whole-book sale coupled with winding down would look like that; a revolving sale where ZFPI keeps the accounts and keeps originating does not. There is also no borrowing, leverage or encumbrance limit on a non-bank card issuer (contrast pawnshops, where BSP caps borrowings at 50% of net worth) — so the secured-loan route (rungs 2–3) is unconstrained too. The monthly Credit Card Business Activity Report continues to show receivables.
  • Securitization Act (RA 9267) — optional, and best avoided. Its §9 BSP-endorsement gate applies when the originator is a BSP-supervised financial intermediary, which ZFPI is — but only if we use the Act. Every operative provision is keyed to a Philippine SEC-registered special-purpose entity, an SEC-approved Plan and the issuance of asset-backed securities; a private bilateral sale to a Singapore or Delaware SPV funded by a fund's loan, with no securities issued, is outside it, and the Act has no exclusivity clause. The trade-off for staying outside: no statutory true-sale safe harbour (§12) and no VAT/DST exemption on the transfer (§§28–31). The bank/quasi-bank securitisation rules (MORNBFI Sec. 622-Q / MORB Sec. 628, which expressly contemplate revolving card structures and need prior BSP approval) sit in the Q- and bank books, not the N- or CC-books, so they do not reach ZFPI unless it is a subsidiary or affiliate of a Philippine bank [counsel to confirm the "optional" reading].
  • Assignment mechanics (Civil Code Arts. 1624–1627; PPSA). Cardholder consent is not required; notice only determines whom the cardholder can safely pay (Ledonio v. Capitol Development, 2007: "not the consent of the debtor, but merely notice"). In practice cardholders keep paying ZFPI as servicer, notice is reserved as a servicer-termination trigger, and until notice the SPV carries commingling risk. Each assignment must be in a public instrument (notarised) to bind third parties (Art. 1625). Under RA 11057 the buyer of receivables is a secured party (§3), anti-assignment clauses in the account terms are ineffective (§10(a)), and perfection is by PPSR registration — no account-debtor notice needed. Avoid drafting that looks like conventional subrogation, which would need cardholder consent (Licaros, 2001).
  • Consumer and data rules — the one place ZFPI's own documents must change. RA 10870 §16 (mirrored at Sec. 101-CC) lists the permitted disclosures of cardholder information — consent in the application/agreement, court order, credit bureaus and networks, service providers "assisting the issuer in enforcing its rights", insurers, fraud prevention. None covers a purchaser or financier of receivables. Disclosure to the SPV, its lender and any backup servicer therefore has to rest on consent written into the cardholder T&Cs ("assignees, transferees, financiers and their agents"). The Data Privacy Act (RA 10173) adds a contract/legitimate-interest basis, keeps ZFPI accountable for data transferred abroad (§21) and has no cross-border approval or adequacy regime — a data-sharing or outsourcing agreement with contractual safeguards suffices [counsel — NPC circular texts not retrieved]. An anonymised loan tape is outside "personal information"; pseudonymised account IDs are not. Circular 1160 (Consumer Protection Act IRR) treats a term letting the issuer "assign the contract to the detriment of the consumer without consent" as unfair — an assignment of receivables that leaves cardholder terms unchanged should not be "to the detriment", but the T&Cs should carry a consumer-neutral assignment clause. Endorsing accounts to a third-party collector triggers RA 10870 §21's seven-business-day cardholder notice; ZFPI servicing SPV-owned receivables does not.
  • Quasi-banking trap (MORNBFI Sec. 201-N). Borrowing from 20 or more lenders to purchase receivables or relend — including via "certificates of assignment with recourse" — is quasi-banking and needs prior BSP authority. One affiliated SPV lender, or a without-recourse sale, is outside it; confirm the SPV borrows for its own account and is not a pass-through for many noteholders. Recourse features also invite recharacterisation of a sale as a loan.
  • The foreign SPV's own status. Under the Foreign Investments Act (RA 7042 §3(d)) the test is "continuity of commercial dealings". An offshore lender with no Philippine presence is not doing business — the FX Manual presupposes non-resident non-bank creditors. A revolving purchaser is weaker: a continuing programme is "continuity", and ZFPI-as-servicer arguably acts for the SPV's account; mitigants are a single counterparty, offshore execution and an undisclosed assignment with ZFPI collecting in its own name [counsel — no SEC opinion retrieved]. The Financing Company Act (RA 8556) defines factoring of receivables as a financing-company activity requiring SEC registration and ≥40% Filipino ownership — a 100%-foreign SPV cannot qualify, so the question collapses into whether it is doing business in the Philippines at all. The SPV is neither an "issuer" nor an "acquirer" under RA 10870, so BSP oversight stays with ZFPI as servicer.
  • FX — the single biggest practical obstacle to a sale. The FX Manual has a clean slot for the loan route (§§22–25: Notice within a month, registration to buy bank FX for debt service; a peso-denominated loan from the SPV qualifies as a "foreign loan" too). It has no provision for the sale of domestic receivables to a non-resident. The nearest fit is registering the purchase as an "other form of investment" (§35, Form W within one year under §36.1(g)), after which the SPV can repatriate collections through banks (§38); unregistered peso collections cannot be converted through banks and would have to leave via non-bank FX or ZFPI's own dollars — legal but operationally weak for a revolving programme, and untested for card receivables [counsel / BSP-IOD confirmation]. Deferred-price or recourse features risk the whole thing being recharacterised as a foreign loan anyway.

What this means for us. The BSP is not the barrier to an SPV true sale; the Philippine tax and FX plumbing is. Outside RA 9267 a sale bears DST on each transfer and VAT or gross receipts tax on the buyer's yield with no exemption, has no statutory true-sale safe harbour, and depends on an FX-registration route the Manual was not written for. That is why no Philippine consumer lender has disclosed one (§9) and why every peer has used the secured-loan shape (rungs 2–3) — which the BSP permits with a one-month Notice and no cap on a card issuer's borrowings. Two things we should do now regardless of route, because they take effect only for new cardholders: add consent to disclosure to "assignees, transferees, financiers and their agents" (RA 10870 §16(a)) and a consumer-neutral assignment clause to the cardholder agreement. Not found: any public Philippine consumer-receivables true sale, any SEC opinion on foreign receivables purchasers, any BSP or NPC guidance specific to this fact pattern.

8. On- vs off-balance-sheet, and the guarantee

  • On-balance-sheet (rungs 2–4, any borrower): fast and cheap to document; no sale, so the cardholder and BSP relationships are untouched; the fund carries our corporate risk as well as asset risk and prices accordingly.
  • Off-balance-sheet (rung 1 only, and only with third-party first loss and ceded control): the fund underwrites the pool, not our burn; debt is non-recourse to the parent. As §2 explains, a captive SPV we service and hold first loss in will consolidate under both IFRS and US GAAP; what we gain is legal isolation and non-recourse treatment, not presentation.
  • The guarantee is the real negotiation. A full holdco guarantee (Accial's ask) gives the isolation benefit back. The US-market standard is a bad-acts guaranty (fraud, servicer misconduct, reps, voluntary bankruptcy) or a payment guaranty capped around 10% of commitment with step-downs; the EM first-facility reality is fuller recourse (§3.2). Whatever its form, the guarantee is reached only after Zed's first-loss slice is exhausted (§2), so the two are priced together: a thinner first loss invites a fuller guarantee and vice versa. On a Delaware-parent-borrower structure the parent guarantee is inherent, so the fight moves to negative pledges and how later parent debt ranks.

11. Our constraints and the recommended posture

What we want (as told to Raymond 9/18): a ~$5M first facility closed before the Series B process (conversations ~end 2026, close mid-2027).

Terms that keep unit economics positive after cost of financing, withholding gross-up and FX hedge — all under the 36% regulatory yield cap; PHP onshore long-term (§9 archetype C is now a proven route); offshore near-term if that gets the first fund in.

Posture

  1. Insist on asset-backed mechanics whoever the borrower is — borrowing base, eligibility criteria, performance triggers, controlled collection accounts — and on rung 2 or 3 collateral perfected on the PPSR. No share-pledge-only structure.
  2. Resist warrants: the product is asset-backed, and warrants can void the portfolio-interest exemption on a Delaware borrower.
  3. Size the structure to a $5M first facility. Default to the Delaware parent as borrower with a secured, mirrored intercompany note assigned to the fund (rung 3) — cheapest on tax (≈15% of the coupon, ~2.3 points of principal a year), no new entity, fastest to close, and consistent with what first facilities at this size actually look like (§3.1). Price the 15% PH withholding on the intercompany coupon into the cost of funds and confirm FPF's vehicle can deliver a clean W-8.
  4. If the lender wants isolation, offer a Delaware LLC SPV before Singapore. Same bankruptcy-remoteness and faster-than-court enforcement, ~$400/yr plus an independent manager, and it keeps the parent's tax profile; on the PH leg let the parent lend to ZFPI directly until counsel clears the treaty point (§7.1). Offer Singapore only where the lender's model requires it (Accial) — it costs ~5 points of the coupon in extra withholding (~$40K/yr on $5M) plus 17% on a thin spread. In either case: no true-sale plumbing at $5M, migration language only.
  5. Negotiate the guarantee's shape, not its existence. Expect recourse to the parent on a first facility (§3.2). Ask for: a cap (the documented US reference is 10% of commitment; expect the lender to open higher), a step-down after 12–18 months of clean borrowing-base compliance, and coverage limited to bad acts plus servicer performance once the cap is hit. Concede a pledge of the SPV's or borrower's equity; refuse a pledge of ZFPI shares and offer direct PPSR-registered receivables security (rung 2) instead.
  6. Build the borrowing-base reporting once so it carries to a PHP bank line (§9 archetype C) when the book and the PPSR track record justify it.
  7. Amend the cardholder T&Cs now — consent to disclosure to assignees, transferees, financiers and their agents (RA 10870 §16(a)) plus a consumer-neutral assignment clause (Circular 1160). Every route needs it, it binds only new cardholders, and it costs nothing (§5.1).
